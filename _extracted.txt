SRT的存在形式
根据存在形式不同，分为Global SRT 与Local SRT 两部分。Global SRT 面向整体 APK，Local SRT 聚焦关键业务逻辑。
对比 | Global SRT | Local SRT
注入位置 | Manifest文件 | 需要保护的业务逻辑或代码相关文件
保护目标 | 干扰对整个 APK 的分析 | 干扰对特定关键逻辑的分析
触发时机 | Agent 开始读取全局信息时 | Agent 分析目标业务代码时
主要优势 | 覆盖范围大，agent在进行关键代码定位时会较早触发 | 保护目标明确，与关键逻辑结合更紧密
主要局限 | 位置明显，容易被针对性识别和移除 | 覆盖范围较窄，效果依赖注入位置
Global SRT 强调覆盖范围，Local SRT 强调目标代码保护；二者形成整体阻断与局部保护的不同策略。

--- Slide 7 ---
SRT的净化/无害化
SRT需要保持触发能力、移除实际危害、保持 APK 功能，因此需要对SRT进行无害化处理。无害化处理不是简单删除敏感语义，而是剥离其可执行性的同时不破坏其触发能力。
一个合格的 SRT，不是危险内容，而是经过净化后仍具有护栏触发特征的性语义文本。

Safety-Refusal Triggers as an Active Defense against
LLM-Assisted Android Reverse Engineering: A Pilot Study
Wenjie Yang†, Wen Li‡, Huming Qiu†, Xiaohan Zhang† , Mi Zhang†, and Min Yang†
†Fudan University, ‡Zhengzhou University
wenjie_yang@m.fudan.edu.cn,xh_zhang@fudan.edu.cn
Abstract
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and AI agents are
accelerating Android reverse engineering by enabling automated
interpretation of decompiled code and recovery of application logic.
However, attackers can abuse these capabilities to discover vul-
nerabilities and recover proprietary code, business logic, or other
security-sensitive implementation details.To protect app code from
this model-facing risk, we propose Safety-Refusal Triggers (SRTs),
an active defense that leverages LLM safety guardrails rather than
increasing code complexity. SSRT construction begins with thesan-
itizationof policy-sensitive source text, which removes details that
could support practical execution while preserving the high-level
topic, hazard, and safety-relevant semantics needed to trigger safety
guardrails. The resulting non-executable, policy-sensitive text is
embedded in an APK artifact. When exposed during LLM-assisted
analysis, they may enter the model context and induce a safety-
related refusal or early termination. We implement a pipeline for
collecting, sanitizing, and injecting SRTs into Android applications.
In a pilot study, we evaluate four fixed SRTs on six open-source An-
droid applications using three LLM-agent configurations. Across 72
manifest-injection runs, 45 end in qualifying safety refusals, yield-
ing a refusal rate of 62.5%. In a text-control ablation, random text
produces 0/24 refusals, compared with 12/24 for unsanitized source
text and 11/24 for sanitized SRTs. These results provide initial evi-
dence of pipeline-level interruption in the evaluated non-adaptive
first-pass setting, but do not establish robustness against adaptive
analysis.
1 Introduction
Reverse engineering remains a persistent challenge for Android
app vendors. Attackers can analyze APKs to understand their code
and recover application logic. The recovered knowledge may en-
able them to discover and exploit vulnerabilities, bypass security
controls, support illicit activities, or copy proprietary code and
business logic, leading to intellectual property infringement and
unfair competition.
To make such analysis more difficult, vendors commonly use
code obfuscation and packing. These techniques increase the effort
required to understand code and recover application logic. Recent
advances in large language models (LLMs) and AI agents, however,
are changing how reverse engineering is performed.
LLMs can analyze source code and obfuscated code, while agentic
systems can operate binary analysis tools and coordinate multi-
step workflows [7, 13]. A controlled human study further shows
that LLM assistance can accelerate function triage and improve
artifact recovery during reverse engineering [2]. Work on Android
likewise studies the use of LLMs for obfuscation analysis [8]. These
capabilities reduce the effort required to understand an unfamiliar
application, allowing attackers to analyze protected APKs more
efficiently and creating a new challenge for app code protection.
Traditional app protection primarily transforms the program
representation exposed to human analysts and conventional anal-
ysis tools. LLM-assisted reverse engineering introduces another
interface: application artifacts are transformed into model context
during analysis. This artifact-to-context channel creates an addi-
tional surface for app protection.
We explore an active defense aimed directly at analysis by LLM-
based agents. Safety-aligned LLMs use guardrails to refuse requests
involving restricted content, such as chemical, biological, radio-
logical, and nuclear (CBRN) content. When an LLM-based agent
analyzes an APK, content extracted from the app may become
part of the model’s context. Our approach embeds sanitized policy-
sensitive content in the APK. When this content is exposed during
analysis, it may activate these guardrails and lead the agent to
refuse or terminate the reverse-engineering workflow. We term
such content aSafety-Refusal Trigger(SRT).
Unlike conventional obfuscation or packing, the proposed mech-
anism does not attempt to make program semantics intrinsically
more difficult to recover. Instead, it targets the artifact-to-context
channel introduced by LLM-assisted analysis and attempts to inter-
rupt the model-facing stage of the reverse-engineering workflow.
We design the defense around three operations: (1)construct-
ing policy-sensitive triggers, (2)removing actionable details while
retaining the semantics needed to activate safety guardrails, and (3)
embedding the resulting content in an APK carrier likely to be ex-
posed during analysis. Our prototype implements these operations
as an automated pipeline for transforming policy-sensitive source
samples intoSRTs and injecting the resultingSRTs into Android
projects.
For the pilot evaluation, we instantiate the design with a 40-item
corpus spanning four CBRN topics and use manifest metadata as
the evaluated carrier. We evaluate four fixed SRT instances on six
real-world Android applications using three LLM-agent configura-
tions. Of 72 analysis runs, 45 terminate in an explicit safety refusal,
yielding an overall refusal rate of 62.5%. Refusals occur across all
three evaluated LLM-agent configurations, although their rates
vary substantially. These results show thatSRTs embedded in appli-
cations can interrupt LLM-assisted reverse-engineering workflows
in the tested non-adaptive setting.
Our ablation study further distinguishes the effect ofSRTs from
the effect of adding arbitrary text. Length-matched random text
produces no safety refusals in 24 runs, whereas sanitizedSRTs
retain 11 of the 12 refusals observed with their unsanitized source
text. These results suggest that the observed effect is associated

2 Problem Statement
LLM-based agents can reduce the effort required to reverse engi-
neer Android applications by interpreting decompiled code and
recovering application logic. This capability creates a new challenge
for app code protection: an attacker can use an APK’s own artifacts
to obtain implementation knowledge more efficiently. App vendors
therefore need mechanisms that can increase the cost of, delay, or
interrupt LLM-assisted reverse engineering.
Threat Model.Figure 1 summarizes the considered threat model.
The attacker obtains a legitimately distributed APK and uses an
LLM-based agent to recover application functionality, business
logic, or security-sensitive implementation details. The defender
is the app vendor that publishes the APK and controls its contents
before distribution, but does not control the attacker’s tools, models,
or analysis environment.
Attacker Capabilities and Scope.The attacker can obtain the APK
and use standard reverse-engineering tools together with an LLM-
based agent. During analysis, the agent may receive artifacts ex-
tracted from the target, such as the manifest, resources, strings, and
decompiled code. In this pilot study, we focus on a non-adaptive
analyst who submits the APK for analysis without first inspecting
or sanitizing its contents. More adaptive workflows may inspect
or filter application content, isolate artifact views, submit code in
segments, modify prompts, remove suspicious metadata, or switch
to another model. These workflows are outside the scope of the
present pilot and constitute a separate robustness problem.
Defender Capabilities.The defender controls the APK before
distribution and may modify the application as part of its normal
build and protection process. Any protection must preserve the
application’s expected behavior and must not attack the analyst’s
environment. Once the APK is distributed, the defender cannot
control how an attacker inspects it, which tools are used, or which
LLM receives the extracted artifacts.
Security Objective and Scope.Our security objective is to increase
the likelihood that an LLM-assisted reverse-engineering workflow
terminates with a safety-related refusal before completing the re-
quested analysis, while preserving the expected behavior of the
protected Android application. We therefore investigate the follow-
ing problem:
Can a defender-controlled, non-executable APK ar-
tifact influence the model-facing stage of an LLM-
assisted reverse-engineering workflow and induce an
observable safety-related interruption?
The present study evaluates this question under a non-adaptive,
first-pass analysis workflow. The attacker is assumed to submit
the application for analysis without first identifying and removing
SRT-bearing artifacts. We do not claim robustness against adaptive
analysts who explicitly sanitize extracted content, remove suspi-
cious metadata, segment the application into smaller analysis units,
modify prompts, or switch to models with different safety policies.
The distinction is important: the present evaluation addresses the
feasibility of the proposed mechanism rather than its robustness
against adaptive countermeasures.
3 Safety-Refusal Trigger
Core Idea. During Android reverse engineering, LLM-based agents
reason over artifacts derived from an APK, including decompiled
code, configuration data, strings, and tool output. These artifacts
may be passed to the model as analysis context. We call this path the
artifact-to-context channel. It provides a surface through which non-
executable content embedded in an APK may influence an agent’s
analysis workflow. An SRT uses this channel by placing policy-
sensitive content in an APK so that it may enter the model context
during analysis and trigger a safety-related refusal or termination.
Definition and Observable Outcome. AnSRTis sanitized,
non-executable, policy-sensitive text embedded in an APK artifact.
It does not rely on code execution or runtime behavior and is not
intended to alter the application’s expected behavior. We consider
an SRT effective for a given LLM, its corresponding agent, and
an analysis protocol when the relevant artifact content enters the

Design Requirements. A useful SRT should satisfy three re-

• Policy relevance.The text should retain sufficient high-level
topic and hazard semantics to engage the target model’s safety

• Non-actionability.The text should exclude operational details,

• Artifact compatibility.The text should remain non-executable,

during the intended analysis workflow.

instructions that direct the agent to ignore the user, change its task,
invoke a tool, or perform a chosen action.
4 Design & Implementation
Our prototype implements SRT deployment in three stages: SRT
collection, SRT sanitization, and SRT injection. Collection produces
policy-sensitive source samples and screens them for the target
safety-related response. Sanitization removes actionable details
and reviews the resulting text to produce accepted SRTs. Injection
places a selected SRT in a configured APK artifact carrier and builds
the resulting APK. The first two stages form an SRT preparation
pipeline, while the injector consumes the prepared SRT records and
carrier configuration. Figure 2 summarizes this workflow.
4.1 SRT Collection
The collection stage begins with policy-sensitive material obtained
from publicly accessible sources, with LLM assistance. We organize
this material into independent, self-contained samples according to
their topics. Each sample is submitted to a predefined target model
under a direct screening protocol. When the model explicitly refuses
to process a sample or terminates its response for a stated safety-
related reason, we include the sample in thepolicy-sensitive source
corpus. These source samples are candidates for further processing
rather than deployable SRTs.
4.2 SRT Sanitization
The sanitization stage processes each accepted source sample to
remove actionable information. The pipeline eliminates ordered pro-
cedures, quantitative parameters, operating conditions, acquisition
guidance, and troubleshooting details, while retaining high-level
topic, hazard, and safety-relevant semantics. The resulting text is
then screened using the same protocol and reviewed for residual
actionability.
A sample is designated as anSRTonly if it retains the target
safety-related response and passes the safety review. Otherwise, it
is revised and reassessed or discarded. Accepted SRTs are assigned
an identifier, category, and version before entering the corpus used
by the injector. This process reduces the dual-use risk of embedded
content while preserving the semantics required by the proposed
mechanism.
In this paper,direct refusal screeningrefers to submitting a can-
didate text under the predefined screening protocol and retaining
it only when the target model explicitly declines to process the
content or terminates its response for a stated safety-related reason.
Warnings, cautions, or safety-oriented commentary followed by
substantive processing of the candidate are not considered qualify-
ing refusals.
Thesafety reviewexamines whether sanitization has removed
information that could materially support harmful use. A candidate
is rejected if it retains ordered procedures, quantitative parameters,
operating conditions, acquisition guidance, troubleshooting instruc-
tions, or comparable operational details. Accepted SRTs retain only
high-level topic, hazard, and safety-relevant semantics.
4.3 SRT Injection
The injection stage takes an accepted SRT and a carrier configu-
ration as input. A suitable carrier is text-bearing, likely to enter
the model context during the intended analysis workflow, non-
executable, and compatible with the application’s expected behav-
ior. The injector resolves the configured location, performs the
configured segmentation and ordering of the SRT text, writes the
resulting content to the Android project, and builds the SRT-injected
APK. SRT construction and carrier configuration are independent,
allowing the same SRT to be placed in different carriers.
4.4 Implementation Details
The SRT preparation pipeline performs screening, sanitization,
screening after sanitization, and safety review in sequence. Only
records that preserve the target safety-related response and pass
the safety review are stored in a versioned UTF-8 JSON corpus
organized by category and identifier. This corpus is the input to the
injector.
We implement the injector as a Kotlin Gradle plugin integrated
into the Android build process. For each build variant, the plu-
gin loads the selected SRT and injection configuration, applies
the configured text layout, validates the configured placement,
and produces an SRT-injected APK. The current implementation
includes a manifest metadata carrier, which parses the merged
AndroidManifest.xml and inserts the SRT as the android:value
of a configurable<meta-data> element under <application>. The
plugin rejects duplicate metadata keys, records SHA-256 hashes

5 Evaluation
We evaluate whether sanitizedSRTs embedded in Android APKs
can trigger safety refusals and interrupt LLM-assisted reverse en-
gineering. Our evaluation focuses on the non-adaptive workflow
defined in Section 2 and measures interruption through an observ-
able safety refusal rather than the eventual success or failure of
reverse engineering.
Research Questions.Guided by the threat model and prototype
design, our evaluation addresses the following research questions:
• RQ1: Refusal effectiveness.Can sanitizedSRTs embed-
ded in manifest metadata induce qualifying safety refusals
in the evaluated non-adaptive LLM-agent analysis work-
flow?
• RQ2: Variation across analysis configurations and se-
lected SRT instances.How do refusal outcomes vary
across the three evaluated LLM-agent configurations and
the four fixed SRT instances used in the pilot evaluation?
• RQ3: Effect of sanitization.How do refusal outcomes
change after sanitization compared with unsanitized source
text, and can the observed effect be distinguished from that
of length-matched random text?
RQ1 is examined through the main experiment, RQ2 through
the cross-configuration and cross-instance comparison, and RQ3
through the text-control and sanitization ablation.
5.1 Evaluation Setup
SRT corpus.Following the construction process in Section 4, we
obtained 40 sanitizedSRTs from 40 policy-sensitive source samples
spanning chemical (C), biological (B), radiological (R), and nuclear
(N) topics, with 10SRTs per topic. CBRN serves as a concrete policy-
sensitive instantiation for this pilot study and provides four distinct
topical groups with which to exercise the SRT construction and
sanitization pipeline.
All 40 sanitized outputs passed the direct refusal screening and
safety review defined in Section 4.2. The 40-item corpus therefore
characterizes the output of the SRT preparation pipeline; it is not

accepted SRT instance from each topic, yielding four evaluated SRT
instances. The downstream experiment is intentionally designed
as a feasibility study of the proposed mechanism rather than a
population-level evaluation of trigger effectiveness within each
CBRN topic. Using a fixed trigger instance for each topic also allows
the trigger content to remain constant while the application and
LLM-agent configuration vary.

Metric.We count a run as asafety refusalwhen the agent explic-
itly declines the requested APK analysis for a safety-related reason
or terminates before completing the task with such a rationale.
Warnings or cautions followed by analysis are non-refusals. Techni-
cal failures, including timeouts, MCP disconnections, parsing errors,
and unexplained product failures, are rerun in a fresh session; we
retain the first valid terminal outcome for each experimental cell.
Our primary metric is theRefusal Rate, defined as the number
of valid runs with a qualifying safety refusal divided by the total
number of valid analysis runs. Valid runs exclude attempts that
terminate solely because of a technical failure. We report descriptive
counts and percentages; because each cell contributes one valid
observation, we do not estimate run-to-run variance or perform
population-level significance tests.
5.2 Results
RQ1: Refusal Effectiveness.Table 4 reports the main result.
Across the six applications, four fixed SRT instances, and three LLM-
agent configurations, 45 of 72 analysis runs ended in a qualifying
safety refusal, for an overall refusal rate of 62.5%. In the evaluated
non-adaptive setting, SRT-bearing manifest metadata therefore
induced observable interruption in a substantial portion of the
tested LLM-assisted reverse-engineering workflows.
Twenty-seven runs did not end in a qualifying safety refusal.
The experiment therefore establishes interruption in the evaluated
workflow, not prevention of reverse engineering: it does not mea-
sure the application logic or other protected information recovered
in non-refusing runs.

induced qualifying safety refusals in 45 of 72 analysis runs, providing
feasibility evidence that safety-mediated interruption can affect LLM-

RQ2: Variation across Analysis Configurations and Selected
SRT Instances.Refusal rates varied substantially across the evalu-
ated LLM-agent configurations, ranging from 8/24 (33.3%) for Grok
4.5 with Codex to 19/24 (79.2%) for Opus 5 with Claude Code.
The four fixed SRT instances also produced different refusal
outcomes across the tested systems. Because only one SRT instance
is evaluated from each CBRN topic, these differences should not
be interpreted as evidence that one CBRN category is inherently
more effective than another. Instead, the results show that observed
refusal behavior can vary with the particular SRT instance and the
LLM-agent analysis configuration.
Differences among GPT-5.6, Opus 5, and Grok 4.5 should likewise
be interpreted at the level of the complete analysis configuration
rather than as effects of the base LLM alone, since provider safety
policies, agent system instructions, context construction, and tool
orchestration may all influence the terminal outcome.
Finding 2.Safety-refusal behavior varies across the evaluated LLM-
agent configurations and individual SRT instances, indicating that the
observed effect is configuration-dependent rather than universal.
RQ3: Effect of Sanitization.The ablation uses Fossify Clock
and Fossify Music Player, the four fixed SRT instances, and the same
three LLM-agent configurations. It compares random non-sensitive
text, unsanitized source text, and sanitizedSRTs. The random-text
control is matched to the corresponding SRT in length, format, and
encoding; it is reviewed as non-actionable and does not elicit a
safety-related response during direct screening. All conditions use
the same manifest carrier, request, tool access, and session protocol,
yielding 24 analysis cells per text condition.
Table 5 reports the text-control and sanitization ablation. Ran-
dom non-sensitive text produced no safety refusals in 24 runs,
whereas unsanitized source text and sanitizedSRTs produced 12/24
(50.0%) and 11/24 (45.8%) refusals, respectively.
Under the evaluated protocol, the length-matched random-text
control suggests that the observed refusal behavior cannot be ex-
plained solely by the presence or length of additional manifest text.
In this sample, sanitization retained 11 of the 12 refusals observed
with unsanitized content while removing the operational details
targeted by the sanitization procedure.
The ablation does not identify the precise semantic features
responsible for refusal, establish statistical equivalence between

6 Discussion
LLMs and Reverse Engineering.LLMs and AI agents can make
reverse engineering more useful for legitimate security work. They
can help analysts interpret decompiled code, triage functions, and
identify implementation details that merit further investigation.
Such assistance can improve vulnerability discovery and remedi-
ation when used by application owners, authorized auditors, or
security researchers.
The same capabilities can lower the cost of recovering propri-
etary application logic when used without authorization. For app
vendors, this creates a code-protection problem that is distinct from
the value of reverse engineering itself.SRTs extend app protection
to the model-facing stage of LLM-assisted analysis by attempting
to interrupt an LLM agent’s analysis of a distributed APK.
The mechanism is complementary to conventional protections
rather than a replacement for them. Obfuscation and packing in-
crease the intrinsic difficulty of recovering program structure or
semantics, whereasSRTs target the artifact-to-context interaction
introduced by LLM-assisted analysis. A practical protection stack
could therefore combine conventional code-level protections with
model-facing defenses, although the present pilot evaluates only
the latter mechanism in isolation.
Adaptive Filtering and SRT Removability.The present study
does not establish robustness against adaptive preprocessing. In
particular, the evaluated manifest carrier is intentionally simple
and observable, prioritizing experimental controllability over con-
cealment. An analyst who recognizes the defense may remove
suspicious metadata or apply deterministic preprocessing before
extracted artifacts reach the downstream LLM. Such strategies fall
outside the non-adaptive threat model evaluated in this study.
An LLM-based filtering stage creates a different interaction be-
cause the filtering model itself must process the embedded content.
It is possible that the filtering model’s own safety mechanisms could
affect this preprocessing step. However, we do not experimentally
evaluate this behavior and therefore do not claim thatSRTs resist
LLM-based filtering. Future work should evaluate filter-aware at-
tackers and investigate less conspicuous, distributed, or redundant
placement ofSRTs across multiple application artifacts.
Ethical Considerations.SRT research is dual use. A mecha-
nism that helps vendors study defenses against unauthorized LLM-
assisted reverse engineering could also impede automated malware
analysis or other legitimate security workflows. We therefore limit
the study to benign open-source applications. The prototype does
not execute a payload, exploit an analysis tool, or attack an ana-
lyst’s environment. We omit operational CBRN text and report only
high-level topic labels and aggregate measurements.
Any public release should be limited to the injector, benign build
inputs, evaluation prompts, and non-actionable examples that pass
an appropriate safety review. Operational or model-specific trigger
text should not be released without a separate risk assessment.
Limitations and Future Work.This paper is a pilot study. Al-
though the constructed corpus contains 40 acceptedSRTs across
four CBRN topics, the downstream APK-level experiment evalu-
ates only four fixed SRT instances, one from each topic, across
six applications and three LLM-agent configurations. The result-
ing measurements therefore establish feasibility for the evaluated
instances rather than estimating the distribution of effectiveness
across the full 40-item corpus or comparing the relative effective-
ness of the four CBRN categories.
The six applications also come from a single open-source appli-
cation family, each experimental cell contributes one valid terminal
outcome, and the source corpus is screened in advance for direct
safety-related responses. We therefore report descriptive measure-
ments rather than population-level statistical conclusions. Future
studies should evaluate larger numbers of trigger instances, re-
peated runs, additional application families, and broader LLM-agent
configurations.
We measure safety refusal as an observable interruption out-
come, not the amount of application logic, vulnerabilities, or other
protected information recovered when analysis continues. Injected
APKs build successfully, but we have not completed systematic run-
time and regression testing. Future studies should combine refusal
measurements with reverse-engineering outcomes at the task level
and application-preservation tests.
The evaluated manifest carrier is visible and can be removed by
an analyst who knows the defense. We do not evaluate adaptive
analysts who filter application content, submit code in segments,
modify prompts, switch models, or use local models without com-
parable safety guardrails. Provider safety policies, system prompts,
model behavior, and context-handling strategies may also change
over time. Future work should therefore evaluate adaptive work-
flows, compare the persistence and removability of different carriers,
and track SRT behavior as relevant analysis systems evolve.
7 Related Work
LLM-assisted reverse engineering.LLMs have been evaluated on
general and obfuscated-code analysis [7], and evidence from a hu-
man study shows that they can improve reverse-engineering triage
and artifact recovery [2]. BINREX demonstrates agentic, tool-driven
binary analysis [13], while work on Android examines obfuscation
analysis with LLMs [8]. Nishizaka et al. characterize how software
protection causes failures of LLM agents in CTF reverse engineer-
ing [14]. Together, these studies establish both the capability of
LLM-assisted analysis and failure modes induced by software pro-
tection. Our work examines an Android artifact controlled by the
defender and intended to induce a specific, observable outcome—a
safety refusal—rather than measuring analysis capability or cata-
loguing agent failures.
Active protection against LLM analysis.CHeaT places traps, cloaks,
and honeytokens in network assets to disrupt or detect LLM agents
for penetration testing [ 1]. WebCloak turns webpages into self-
protecting assets through structural obfuscation and harmless se-
mantic misdirection [10]. Prompt injection has also been used defen-
sively to reduce extraction of personal information with LLMs [12].
These systems show that an asset can influence the model that ana-
lyzes it, but they target network penetration testing, web scraping,
6

--- Page 7 ---
or information extraction.SRTs apply this principle to Android re-
verse engineering and evaluate a non-executable APK carrier whose
intended effect is refusal triggered by safety guardrails rather than
deception or adversarial instructions.
Manipulation through analyzed content.Prior work shows that
content encountered during analysis can influence LLM-based sys-
tems. Prompt injection can redirect an application integrated with
an LLM through content controlled by an attacker [11]. Crawford et
al. show that strings recovered by reverse-engineering tools can ma-
nipulate analysis agents [5], while LogInject demonstrates passive
manipulation through security logs [9]. Acoda applies semantics-
preserving obfuscations to induce refusal or misinterpretation [15],
whereas Familiar Pattern Attacks use small code edits that exploit
abstraction bias to induce incorrect interpretation [3]. These stud-
ies motivate treating extracted application content as part of the
model’s analysis surface.SRTs avoid instructions that redirect the
task and do not depend on semantic misanalysis; instead, they ex-
pose sanitized policy-sensitive data to an existing safety boundary.
Safety refusal and anti-analysis.XSTest and OR-Bench docu-
ment over-refusal on benign prompts that resemble unsafe re-
quests [6, 16]. These benchmarks treat refusal as a model behavior
to measure, rather than as a software-protection mechanism. A
security report describes malicious software packages containing
non-executing content intended to trigger safety policies and dis-
rupt AI-assisted scanners [4], but provides no controlled evaluation.
Our pilot studies this failure surface in a controlled Android reverse-
engineering setting with benign apps, sanitized triggers, multiple
analysis configurations, and explicit controls.
Taken together, prior work has studied LLM-assisted reverse
engineering, manipulation through analyzed content, and safety
refusal in largely separate lines of work.SRTs connect these di-
rections by using safety refusal as a mechanism controlled by the
defender for app protection.
8 Conclusion
LLMs and AI agents can lower the cost of recovering implementa-
tion knowledge from Android applications, creating a code-protection
challenge for application vendors. We propose Safety-Refusal Trig-
gers (SRTs), an active defense that embeds sanitized, policy-sensitive
text in an APK and targets the artifact-to-context channel used by
LLM-assisted reverse-engineering workflows.
Experiments with three LLM-agent configurations on six be-
nign Android applications show thatSRTs can induce observable
safety-related interruption in the evaluated non-adaptive setting,
with 45 of 72 main analysis runs ending in qualifying refusals. The
random-text control further suggests that the observed behavior
is associated with the safety-sensitive semantics of the injected
content rather than merely the presence or length of additional
manifest text.
These results establish the feasibility of safety-mediated interrup-
tion as a complementary Android app-protection primitive. They
do not establish robustness against adaptive filtering or general
prevention of reverse engineering. Future work should evaluate
broader trigger populations, more persistent and less conspicu-
ous artifact carriers, repeated analysis runs, and adaptive analysis
strategies.
